Wave function and continuous space

An electron does not have two possible positions; it has infinitely many. The ket becomes a function, sums become integrals, and position and momentum turn out to be two faces of the same object.

  • Normalise a wave function and compute the probability of finding the particle in an interval.
  • Compute \(\langle x\rangle\), \(\Delta x\) and, by Fourier transform, the momentum distribution.
  • Derive \([X, P] = i\hbar\) from translations and deduce \(\Delta x\,\Delta p \ge \hbar/2\).
  • Predict the motion and spreading of a free wave packet, and estimate a tunnelling probability.

Read the transcript

For a quantum object moving along a line, the state becomes a function, psi of x. The squared modulus of psi is a probability density: the probability of finding the particle between a and b is the area under this curve.

The same information can be written in terms of momentum. We go from one to the other with a Fourier transform. A packet that is narrow in position is broad in momentum, and the other way round. For a Gaussian packet, the product of the two standard deviations is exactly h-bar over two: the minimum allowed by the uncertainty relation. That relation follows directly from the commutator of position and momentum, which equals i h-bar.

The evolution is given by the Schrödinger equation. A free packet moves at the group velocity, p over m, and spreads out, because its different components do not travel at the same speed. For an electron localised within one nanometre, the width doubles in about thirty femtoseconds. For a one-gram marble, it would take far longer than the age of the Universe.

Faced with a barrier higher than its energy, a particle can still get through. This is tunnelling, and the scanning tunnelling microscope relies on it.

5.1The wave function and the presence cloud

In 1924, Louis de Broglie proposed that a material particle is accompanied by a wave. Confirmation came in 1927 at Bell Laboratories: Clinton Davisson and Lester Germer fired electrons at a nickel crystal and observed a diffraction pattern. Their sample had recrystallised by accident after being heated, which made the diffraction visible.

For a qubit, the state is expanded on two kets \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\). For a quantum object on a line, it is expanded on infinitely many kets \(\ket x\), one per position. The rules stay the same, with three substitutions:

  • the sum \(\sum_i\) becomes an integral \(\int \mathrm dx\);
  • the Kronecker delta \(\delta_{ij}\) becomes the Dirac delta: \(\braket{x'|x} = \delta(x'-x)\);
  • the closure relation \(\sum_i \ket i\bra i = \mathbb 1\) becomes \(\int \mathrm dx\,\ket x\bra x = \mathbb 1\).

The kets \(\ket x\) cannot be normalised: they are computational tools, not states that can be prepared. The property of \(\delta\) to remember is \(\int_{x_1}^{x_2} f(x)\,\delta(x - x_0)\,\mathrm dx = f(x_0)\) if \(x_0 \in [x_1, x_2]\), and 0 otherwise.

Definition 5.1 — Wave function

The wave function is the component of the ket in the position basis:

$$\psi(x) = \braket{x|\psi},\qquad \ket\psi = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} \mathrm dx\,\psi(x)\ket x.$$

It is a probability density amplitude, complex in general, and square-integrable (\(\psi\in L^2(\mathbb R)\)). The lecture notes call it the “state function”; the usual name “wave function” comes from the historical comparison with classical waves.

Principle 5.1 — Born rule in the continuum

\(|\psi(x)|^2\) is a probability density (unit: m−1). The probability of finding the quantum object in \([a, b]\) is

$$P([a,b]) = \int_a^b |\psi(x)|^2\,\mathrm dx,\qquad \text{with}\quad \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} |\psi(x)|^2\,\mathrm dx = 1.$$

The probability of finding the quantum object at one exact point is zero. Only a probability over an interval is meaningful.

Mean and spread are computed as for any statistical distribution: \(\langle x\rangle = \int x\,|\psi|^2\,\mathrm dx\) and \((\Delta x)^2 = \langle x^2\rangle - \langle x\rangle^2\).

Common pitfall — amplitude or probability?

\(\psi(x)\) is not a probability: it is a complex number that carries a phase. Two wave functions can have the same density \(|\psi|^2\) and different phases, hence different interference and different momentum distributions (lab 5.2).

Exercise 5.2

The middle third of the box

★★☆

A quantum object is in the state \(\psi(x) = \sqrt{2/L}\,\sin(\pi x/L)\) on \([0, L]\) (zero elsewhere). Check the normalisation, then compute the probability of finding it between \(L/3\) and \(2L/3\) (three decimal places).

Solution

An antiderivative of \(\tfrac2L\sin^2(\pi x/L)\) is \(\tfrac xL - \tfrac{1}{2\pi}\sin(2\pi x/L)\). On \([0, L]\) it gives 1: the function is normalised. On \([L/3, 2L/3]\): \(\tfrac13 - \tfrac{1}{2\pi}\big(\sin\tfrac{4\pi}3 - \sin\tfrac{2\pi}3\big) = \tfrac13 + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2\pi} \approx 0.609\). On the first quarter \([0, L/4]\), one finds \(\tfrac14 - \tfrac1{2\pi} \approx 0.091\).

Analogy to critique — the cloud

The lecture notes compare \(|\psi(x)|^2\) to the density of a cloud: thick where the quantum object is likely to be found, transparent elsewhere.

Where does the analogy break down?

A real cloud is made of droplets, each of which has a position: its blur comes from our ignorance of the details. The blur of \(|\psi|^2\) is not of this kind: before measurement, the quantum object has no hidden position that we would simply need to read off. Another difference: two clouds that meet add up, whereas two amplitudes can cancel.

5.2The other face: the momentum representation

We can just as well expand \(\ket\psi\) on the kets \(\ket p\) of definite momentum, with \(\braket{p'|p} = \delta(p'-p)\) and \(\int\mathrm dp\,\ket p\bra p = \mathbb 1\). The component \(\varphi(p) = \braket{p|\psi}\) is the wave function in momentum space. To go from one to the other, all we need is the plane wave \(\braket{x|p} = e^{ipx/\hbar}/\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}\).

Principle 5.2 — The two faces are linked by Fourier
$$\varphi(p) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\mathrm dx\,\psi(x)\,e^{-ipx/\hbar},$$
$$\psi(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\mathrm dp\,\varphi(p)\,e^{ipx/\hbar}.$$

\(|\varphi(p)|^2\) is the momentum probability density, and the norm is the same on both sides (Plancherel’s theorem): \(\int|\psi|^2\,\mathrm dx = \int|\varphi|^2\,\mathrm dp = 1\).

For the Gaussian \(\psi(x) = (\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/4}e^{-x^2/2\sigma^2}\), completing the square in the exponent gives another Gaussian:

$$\varphi(p) = \left(\frac{\sigma^2}{\pi\hbar^2}\right)^{1/4} e^{-p^2\sigma^2/2\hbar^2}.$$

The smaller \(\sigma\), the broader \(\varphi\): a sharply localised quantum object has a poorly defined momentum.

Clarification

The lecture notes write \(\sigma_x\sigma_p = \hbar\), taking \(\sigma\) and \(\hbar/\sigma\) as “widths”. With standard deviations, which are the ones in the Heisenberg relation, \(|\psi|^2 \propto e^{-x^2/\sigma^2}\) gives \(\Delta x = \sigma/\sqrt2\) and \(|\varphi|^2\propto e^{-p^2\sigma^2/\hbar^2}\) gives \(\Delta p = \hbar/(\sigma\sqrt2)\). The product is exactly \(\hbar/2\): the Gaussian reaches the bound. On the corresponding slide, the constant \((1/\pi\sigma^2)^{1/4}\) accompanies \(e^{-x^2/4\sigma^2}\): with that exponential, \((1/2\pi\sigma^2)^{1/4}\) is needed for the state to be normalised.

Analogy to critique — the Nommo twins of Dogon cosmogony

The lecture notes suggest comparing the position/momentum pair with the principle of twinness in Dogon cosmogony (Mali): two opposite and complementary principles, both needed for the balance of the world.

Where does the analogy break down?

First, a correction: in the accounts collected among the Dogon, Amma is the creator god, and the Nommo are the twin beings he begets. Amma and Nommo therefore do not form a pair of twins, contrary to what the lecture notes suggest. Second, quantum complementarity is quantitative: \(\psi\) and \(\varphi\) contain exactly the same information, linked by a Fourier transform, and the bound \(\Delta x\,\Delta p \ge \hbar/2\) can be calculated. A mythological duality provides neither this equivalence nor this bound.

5.3Operators in continuous space

“Where are you?”: the position operator multiplies the wave function by \(x\), \((X\psi)(x) = x\,\psi(x)\). Its eigenstates are the \(\delta(x - x_0)\).

“Where are you going?”: momentum is defined by its geometric role. The translation operator \(T(a)\) shifts the state, \((T(a)\psi)(x) = \psi(x-a)\). For a small displacement, \(T(\mathrm da) \approx \mathbb 1 - \tfrac{i\,\mathrm da}{\hbar}P\), so \(T(a) = e^{-iaP/\hbar}\). Expanding \(\psi(x - \mathrm da) \approx \psi(x) - \mathrm da\,\psi'(x)\) gives the differential form.

Definitions 5.3 — Position, momentum, commutator
$$(X\psi)(x) = x\,\psi(x),\qquad (P\psi)(x) = -i\hbar\,\frac{\mathrm d\psi}{\mathrm dx},\qquad [X, P] = i\hbar\,\mathbb 1.$$

The commutation relation follows from \(T^\dagger(a)\,X\,T(a) = X + a\,\mathbb 1\) to first order in \(a\). It implies, for every state, \(\Delta x\,\Delta p \ge \hbar/2\).

The expectation values are \(\langle X\rangle = \int x\,|\psi|^2\,\mathrm dx\) and \(\langle P\rangle = \int \psi^*(x)\,(-i\hbar\,\psi'(x))\,\mathrm dx\). The uncertainty is not a flaw of the instruments: no state gives narrow distributions in both \(x\) and \(p\) at the same time.

5.4Dynamics of the wave function

Principle 5.4 — Schrödinger equation
$$i\hbar\,\frac{\partial\psi(x,t)}{\partial t} = \left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2} + V(x)\right)\psi(x,t).$$

It is first order in time (the initial state fixes the whole future, between two measurements) and linear (any superposition of solutions is a solution).

A state of definite energy \(E_n\) evolves by a mere phase, \(\psi_n(x,t) = \phi_n(x)\,e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}\), where \(\phi_n\) satisfies the time-independent equation \(-\tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\phi_n'' + V\phi_n = E_n\phi_n\). Its density \(|\psi_n|^2 = |\phi_n|^2\) does not move. “Stationary” does not mean “motionless”: a plane wave \(e^{i(kx - \omega t)}\) has a uniform, constant density, yet it carries a probability current.

Tunnelling

Facing a barrier of height \(V_0\) greater than its energy \(E\), a classical ball turns back. The wave function, however, does not drop abruptly to zero: it decays as \(e^{-\kappa x}\) inside the barrier, with \(\kappa = \sqrt{2m(V_0 - E)}/\hbar\). If the barrier is thin enough, part of the wave emerges on the other side. For a rectangular barrier of width \(a\) and \(E < V_0\):

$$T(E) = \left[1 + \frac{V_0^2\,\sinh^2(\kappa a)}{4E(V_0 - E)}\right]^{-1},$$

which, for a thick barrier (\(\kappa a \gg 1\)), becomes

$$T(E) \approx 16\,\frac{E}{V_0}\Big(1 - \frac{E}{V_0}\Big)e^{-2\kappa a}.$$
Application — the scanning tunnelling microscope

A metal tip scans a conducting surface a few ångströms away. The vacuum between them acts as the barrier, with \(V_0 - E\) equal to the work function, about 4.5 eV. The current is proportional to \(e^{-2\kappa L}\): it depends so strongly on the distance \(L\) that individual atoms can be distinguished.

The exercise in the lecture notes on the blue LED (3 nm well, transition \(n = 1 \to n = 0\)) leads to an effective mass of about \(0.045\,m_e\). Analogy to critique — the master drummer

The lecture notes compare stationary states to the rhythms that the master drummer of Yoruba or Akan traditions keeps up throughout a ceremony: stable, recognisable “modes”. More concretely, a stretched drumhead vibrates in a lasting way only in certain normal modes, each with its own frequency.

Where does the analogy break down?

The modes of a drumhead are real and visible: the skin moves. In a stationary state, nothing observable moves; only a global phase rotates, with no measurable effect. The comparison with normal modes holds; the one with ritual rhythms is symbolic and predicts nothing. Moreover, a rhythm is a sequence of events in time, whereas a stationary state is precisely what does not change in time.

5.5A wave packet in motion

A plane wave has a perfectly defined momentum but extends over all space; a Dirac delta is perfectly localised but contains every momentum. A real quantum object lies in between: a wave packet, a superposition of plane waves with neighbouring momenta that interfere constructively in a small region. For a free quantum object, each component evolves with \(E(p) = p^2/2m\):

$$\psi(x,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\int\mathrm dp\,\varphi(p)\,e^{i(px - E(p)t)/\hbar}.$$
Definitions 5.5 — Two velocities, one spreading

The group velocity, that of the envelope, is \(v_g = \mathrm dE/\mathrm dp = p_0/m\): it is the classical velocity. The phase velocity, that of the crests of the carrier wave, is \(v_\varphi = E/p = p_0/2m = v_g/2\). For a Gaussian packet of initial width \(\Delta x_0\) (standard deviation):

$$\Delta x(t) = \Delta x_0\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{t}{t_0}\right)^2},$$
$$t_0 = \frac{2m\,(\Delta x_0)^2}{\hbar}.$$
Clarification

The lecture notes attribute the spreading to the fact that the phase velocity \(p/2m\) depends on \(p\). What matters is that the components of the packet do not share the same group velocity \(p/m\): in a time \(t\), they separate by about \(\Delta p\,t/m\). This recovers the long-time behaviour, \(\Delta x(t) \approx \Delta p\,t/m\) with \(\Delta p = \hbar/(2\Delta x_0)\).

In exercise 5.6.1 of the lecture notes, if \(\Delta x_0\) and \(\Delta k\) are standard deviations (\(\Delta x_0\,\Delta k = 1/2\)), the term under the square root reads \(\hbar t/(2m\Delta x_0^2) = 2\hbar(\Delta k)^2t/m\), not \(\hbar(\Delta k)^2t/2m\). The spreading time is then \(t_0 = 2m\Delta x_0^2/\hbar = m/(2\hbar\Delta k^2)\).

Finally, the sentence “no action smaller than \(\hbar/2\) is observed” does not follow from \(\Delta x\,\Delta p \ge \hbar/2\). This inequality concerns the standard deviations of a single state, not a minimum action.

5.6Review exercises

Exercise 5.10

From the Gaussian to Heisenberg

★★★

Start from \(\psi(x) = (\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/4}e^{-x^2/2\sigma^2}e^{ip_0x/\hbar}\).

  1. Show that \(\langle P\rangle = p_0\) and that \(\Delta x = \sigma/\sqrt2\).
  2. Compute \(\varphi(p)\) by completing the square, then \(\Delta p\).
  3. Check that \(\Delta x\,\Delta p = \hbar/2\). What happens to \(\Delta p\) over time for a free quantum object? And to \(\Delta x\)?
Hint

The factor \(e^{ip_0x/\hbar}\) simply shifts \(\varphi\): \(\varphi(p) \to \varphi(p - p_0)\). For the last question, look at how each component \(\varphi(p)\) evolves in time.

Solution

\(P\psi = -i\hbar\psi' = \big(p_0 + i\hbar x/\sigma^2\big)\psi\); the second term is odd in \(x\) and averages to zero, hence \(\langle P\rangle = p_0\). One finds \(\varphi(p) = (\sigma^2/\pi\hbar^2)^{1/4}e^{-(p-p_0)^2\sigma^2/2\hbar^2}\), so \(|\varphi|^2 \propto e^{-(p-p_0)^2\sigma^2/\hbar^2}\) and \(\Delta p = \hbar/(\sigma\sqrt2)\). The product is \(\hbar/2\).

For a free quantum object, \(\varphi(p,t) = \varphi(p,0)\,e^{-ip^2t/2m\hbar}\): only the phase changes, so \(|\varphi(p,t)|^2\) and \(\Delta p\) stay constant. On the other hand, \(\Delta x(t)\) grows (lab 5.5), and the product \(\Delta x\,\Delta p\) exceeds \(\hbar/2\) as soon as \(t > 0\).

·Where do things stand in 2026?

2025 Nobel Prize in Physics: tunnelling goes macroscopic

John Clarke, Michel Devoret and John Martinis received the prize “for the discovery of macroscopic quantum mechanical tunnelling and energy quantisation in an electric circuit”. In their experiments at Berkeley in the mid-1980s, what crossed the barrier was not an electron, as in lab 5.4, but a collective variable of a superconducting circuit with a Josephson junction. These circuits are the direct ancestors of the superconducting qubits of Google and IBM.

Nobel Committee press release, 7 October 2025 — nobelprize.org
Matter waves to measure gravity

Commercial absolute gravimeters make the matter waves of laser-cooled atoms interfere. They measure \(g\) to within about \(10^{-8}\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\) and operate continuously in the field. One example is the AQG from Exail (France), which grew out of the LP2N and LNE-SYRTE laboratories. The atomic wave packets are split and then recombined, like the photons in the Mach–Zehnder interferometer of chapter 1.

Exail product sheet, accessed October 2026
Clocks: from the atom to the nucleus

NIST’s single aluminium-ion clock reaches a relative systematic uncertainty of \(5.5\times10^{-19}\) (Phys. Rev. Lett., July 2025). Very recently, two teams (TU Wien and China) operated the first nuclear clocks, locked to a transition of thorium-229 near 148 nm; for now their stability remains below that of the best atomic clocks (Nature, 7 October 2026). A redefinition of the second is not expected before the 2030 General Conference on Weights and Measures.
