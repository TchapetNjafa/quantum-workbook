Measurement and operators

In nineteen twenty-two, Stern and Gerlach sent silver atoms through a non-uniform magnetic field. Instead of one continuous smear, they found two spots. The magnetic moment is quantised. Only after nineteen twenty-five did physicists understand that this is spin.

Place two devices in a row, one along z and the other along x: each output receives half of the atoms. Add a third device along z, and you find half and half again. The measurement along x has erased the information about z.

In quantum mechanics, every measurable quantity is represented by a Hermitian operator. Its eigenvalues are the only possible results. Its eigenvectors are the states after the measurement. The probability of a result is the squared modulus of the projection of the state onto the corresponding eigenvector.

Two operators that do not commute describe incompatible quantities. The product of their standard deviations is bounded below by half the mean value of their commutator. This bound depends on the state. It is the Robertson inequality, and the Heisenberg relation is a special case of it.

2.1Measurement and the verdict of Stern and Gerlach

In classical physics, to measure is to read a value that already exists. In 1922, in Frankfurt, Otto Stern and Walther Gerlach sent a beam of silver atoms between the poles of a magnet whose field is not uniform. Each atom carries a small magnetic moment. If these moments could point in every direction, a continuous band would appear on the plate. Two spots are observed.

  • The projection of the spin onto the magnet’s axis takes only two values: \(S_z = +\hbar/2\) or \(S_z = -\hbar/2\).
  • We cannot predict which spot a given atom will reach: only the frequency (here 50/50) is reproducible.

The silver deposit was too thin to be seen. According to Stern’s account, it appeared when he leaned over the plate while smoking a cheap cigar: the sulphur in the smoke blackened the silver into silver sulphide. Gerlach sent Bohr a postcard with the photograph of the two spots. Both saw in it the “space quantisation” of the old Bohr–Sommerfeld theory. The electron spin was only proposed in 1925, by Uhlenbeck and Goudsmit, and it is the spin that explains the two silver spots.

Principle 2.1 — Quantisation of measurement

A measurement can only give certain well-defined values as its result, which we shall call the eigenvalues of the associated operator. Just after the measurement, the system is in the state associated with the result obtained: this is the reduction (or projection) of the state.

Clarification

Spin or magnetic moment? The lecture notes write “\(\mu_\pm = \pm\hbar/2\)”. What equals \(\pm\hbar/2\) is the projection of the spin \(S_z\), a quantity expressed in J·s. The associated magnetic moment is about \(\mp\mu_B\) (the Bohr magneton, in J/T): for an electron it has the opposite sign.

Discrete, not always. The spin eigenvalues are discrete, but those of position or momentum form a continuum (chapter 5). The principle concerns eigenvalues, not whether they are discrete.

The oven produces a mixture. The atoms leaving the oven are not all in one and the same superposition \(\alpha_+\ket{+} + \alpha_-\ket{-}\). A pure state of this kind points in a definite direction on the Bloch sphere, and would give 100% in a device oriented along that direction. The oven beam gives 50/50 whatever the orientation: it is a statistical mixture, the distinction seen in chapter 1.

Successive measurements: hide and seek

A Stern–Gerlach device with one output blocked becomes a filter: it prepares atoms in a known state. We write \(\mathrm{SG}_\theta\) for a device oriented at angle \(\theta\) from the \(z\) axis, in the \(xz\) plane. For an atom prepared in \(\ket{+}_{\theta_1}\), the probability of leaving through the \(+\) output of a device \(\mathrm{SG}_{\theta_2}\) is

$$P(+_{\theta_2} \leftarrow +_{\theta_1}) = |\braket{+_{\theta_2}|+_{\theta_1}}|^2 = \cos^2\frac{\theta_2 - \theta_1}{2}.$$

Clarification

In the long version of the lecture notes, Einstein speaks here of “spooky action at a distance”. That phrase concerns two entangled, distant particles (the EPR paper of 1935, chapter 4). It does not apply to successive measurements on a single atom. Bell-type experiments have since ruled out local hidden variables.

Analogy to critique — consulting the diviner

Before the consultation, the future is a set of possibilities. The divination practice “forces” the revelation of a single path, just as measurement forces a single eigenvalue. Superposition ↔ open future; measurement ↔ consultation; eigenstate ↔ announced path.

Where does the analogy break down?

A quantum measurement is a physical interaction: it depends neither on the intention nor on the beliefs of the experimenter, and two identical devices give the same statistics. Quantum probabilities can be calculated (Born rule) and checked on a large number of atoms, which no consultation allows. Finally, the atom has no “destiny” to be read: before the measurement along \(x\), there was no value of \(S_x\) to reveal.

2.2Formalism of linear operators

Principle 2.2 — Physical quantity and Hermitian operator

Every measurable quantity \(A\) (energy, spin, position…) is associated with a Hermitian linear operator \(\hat A\) acting on the state space. Over a large number of systems prepared in \(\ket\psi\), the mean of the results is

$$\langle A\rangle_\psi = \bra\psi\hat A\ket\psi.$$

Linearity, \(\hat A(\alpha\ket{\psi_1} + \beta\ket{\psi_2}) = \alpha\hat A\ket{\psi_1} + \beta\hat A\ket{\psi_2}\), is what makes the operator compatible with the superposition principle.

Definition 2.2 — Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

The solutions of \(\hat A\ket{\varphi_i} = a_i\ket{\varphi_i}\) give the possible results \(a_i\) of the measurement and the states \(\ket{\varphi_i}\) of the system just after \(a_i\) has been obtained. The probability of obtaining \(a_i\) is \(P(a_i) = |\braket{\varphi_i|\psi}|^2\) (non-degenerate eigenvalue).

An example: the Z operator

\(\hat Z = \ket0\bra0 - \ket1\bra1\) acts on \(\ket\psi = \alpha\ket0 + \beta\ket1\) to give \(\alpha\ket0 - \beta\ket1\). So \(\hat Z\ket0 = +\ket0\) and \(\hat Z\ket1 = -\ket1\): eigenvalues \(\pm1\), eigenvectors \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\). In quantum computing, this is the “phase-flip” gate. In an orthonormal basis \(\{\ket i\}\), the operator is described by the matrix \(A_{ij} = \bra i\hat A\ket j\). For the NOT gate, \(\hat X\ket0 = \ket1\) and \(\hat X\ket1 = \ket0\) give \(X_{01} = X_{10} = 1\), \(X_{00} = X_{11} = 0\), that is \(\hat X = \ket0\bra1 + \ket1\bra0\).

Spin along an arbitrary axis

For an axis \(\vec n = (\sin\theta\cos\varphi, \sin\theta\sin\varphi, \cos\theta)\), the operator \(\hat\sigma_n = \vec n\cdot\vec{\hat\sigma} = n_x\hat\sigma_x + n_y\hat\sigma_y + n_z\hat\sigma_z\) is

$$\hat\sigma_n = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & e^{-i\varphi}\sin\theta\\ e^{i\varphi}\sin\theta & -\cos\theta\end{pmatrix},\qquad \ket{n_+} = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\frac\theta2\\ e^{i\varphi}\sin\frac\theta2\end{pmatrix},\qquad \ket{n_-} = \begin{pmatrix}\sin\frac\theta2\\ -e^{i\varphi}\cos\frac\theta2\end{pmatrix}.$$

Its eigenvalues are \(\pm1\) for every axis. For a state with Bloch vector \(\vec r\), one can show that \(\langle\hat\sigma_n\rangle = \vec r\cdot\vec n\), hence \(P(+1) = \tfrac12(1 + \vec r\cdot\vec n)\).

2.3Operator algebra

Definitions 2.3 — Adjoint, Hermitian, unitary

Adjoint: \((A^\dagger)_{ij} = A_{ji}^*\), the conjugate transpose. We have \((AB)^\dagger = B^\dagger A^\dagger\) and \((\lambda A)^\dagger = \lambda^* A^\dagger\). For an expression in Dirac notation: reverse the order, swap kets and bras, conjugate the numbers. Example: \((2\ket0\bra1 - i\ket1\bra0)^\dagger = 2\ket1\bra0 + i\ket0\bra1\).

Hermitian: \(A^\dagger = A\). The diagonal elements are real, and elements placed symmetrically about the diagonal are complex conjugates: \(\begin{pmatrix}a & c\\ c^* & b\end{pmatrix}\), \(a, b\in\mathbb R\).

Unitary: \(U^\dagger U = UU^\dagger = \mathbb 1\). It preserves the norm; the evolution of an isolated system is unitary (chapter 3).

A Hermitian operator has real eigenvalues, and its eigenvectors belonging to distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal. It can then be written as a sum of projectors; this is the spectral decomposition:

$$\hat A = \sum_i a_i\,\hat P_i,\qquad \hat P_i = \ket{\varphi_i}\bra{\varphi_i},\qquad \hat P_i^2 = \hat P_i,\qquad \sum_i\hat P_i = \mathbb 1.$$

The trace \(\mathrm{Tr}\,A = \sum_i A_{ii} = \sum_i a_i\) does not depend on the basis, and \(\mathrm{Tr}(AB) = \mathrm{Tr}(BA)\).

Formula sheet — Pauli matrices
$$\sigma_x = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad \sigma_y = \begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix},\quad \sigma_z = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \sigma_j\sigma_k = \delta_{jk}\mathbb 1 + i\varepsilon_{jkl}\sigma_l.$$

Hermitian and unitary (\(\sigma_j^2 = \mathbb 1\)), traceless, with determinant \(-1\) and eigenvalues \(\pm1\). Commutators: \([\sigma_x, \sigma_y] = 2i\sigma_z\) and cyclic permutations. Anticommutators: \(\{\sigma_j, \sigma_k\} = 2\delta_{jk}\mathbb 1\). The spin follows from \(\hat S_j = \frac\hbar2\sigma_j\), hence \([\hat S_x, \hat S_y] = i\hbar\hat S_z\).

\(\varepsilon_{jkl}\) equals \(+1\) for \((x,y,z)\) and its cyclic permutations, \(-1\) for odd permutations (one exchange of two indices), and \(0\) if two indices are equal.

2.4Incompatibility and uncertainty

Definition 2.4 — Commutator and compatibility

\([\hat A, \hat B] = \hat A\hat B - \hat B\hat A\). Two observables that commute have a common basis of eigenvectors: there are states in which both results are certain, and measuring \(A\), then \(B\), then \(A\) gives the first result again. They are said to be compatible.

Method — computing the probabilities of a measurement

1. Write the state \(\ket\psi\). 2. Identify the operator \(\hat A\). 3. Find its eigenvalues \(a_i\) and eigenvectors \(\ket{\varphi_i}\). 4. Project: \(c_i = \braket{\varphi_i|\psi}\). 5. \(P(a_i) = |c_i|^2\), and check that \(\sum_i P(a_i) = 1\).

The Robertson inequality

The standard deviation of a measurement in the state \(\ket\psi\) is \(\Delta A = \sqrt{\langle A^2\rangle - \langle A\rangle^2}\). For any two observables (Robertson, 1929, generalising Heisenberg, 1927):

$$\Delta A\,\Delta B \ \ge\ \frac12\,\big|\bra\psi[\hat A, \hat B]\ket\psi\big|.$$

For the qubit, \(\sigma_j^2 = \mathbb 1\) gives \(\Delta\sigma_j = \sqrt{1 - r_j^2}\), and the inequality for \(\sigma_x, \sigma_y\) becomes \(\Delta\sigma_x\,\Delta\sigma_y \ge |\langle\sigma_z\rangle| = |r_z|\). For position and momentum, \([\hat x, \hat p] = i\hbar\) gives \(\Delta x\,\Delta p \ge \hbar/2\) (chapter 5).

Clarification

The lecture notes state that, if \([\hat A, \hat B] \neq 0\), “it is impossible to prepare a state in which both spreads are zero”. The inequality does not say this: its bound depends on the state and can vanish. For angular momentum, \([\hat L_x, \hat L_y] = i\hbar\hat L_z \neq 0\), and yet the state \(\ell = 0\) gives \(\Delta L_x = \Delta L_y = 0\). Two operators that do not commute can share a few eigenvectors, but not a complete basis. For \(\sigma_x\) and \(\sigma_y\), they share none: the sum \(\Delta\sigma_x^2 + \Delta\sigma_y^2 = 1 + r_z^2\) is always at least 1.

Another subtlety: the Robertson inequality concerns standard deviations measured on copies of the same state. It does not describe the “disturbance” of one measurement by the next.

Application — BB84, incompatibility as an alarm

Alice sends qubits prepared at random in the Z or X basis; Bob measures each one in a randomly chosen basis. They then compare their bases publicly and keep only the bits where the bases agree. An eavesdropper who measures in the wrong basis projects the state: if Bob then measures in Alice’s basis, he gets the wrong bit half of the time. On the bits that are intercepted and kept, this gives an expected error rate of 25%.

Analogy to critique — the sacred mask

In many cultures, a sacred mask sleeps outside ceremonies; its identity shows itself only during the ritual that “activates” it. Sleeping mask ↔ quantity not defined before measurement; ritual ↔ measurement; possible manifestations of the spirit ↔ eigenvalues.

Where does the analogy break down?

The ritual follows rules set by a community and depends on the intentions of the participants. Quantum measurement, by contrast, is a physical interaction that gives the same statistics whoever the human operator is. Above all, the analogy says nothing about incompatibility: a mask can show several qualities at once, whereas \(S_x\) and \(S_z\) cannot be defined together.

Exercise 2.9

Three devices, one angle

★★★

Atoms in \(\ket{+_z}\) (already filtered by SG₁) pass through SG₂ oriented at \(\theta = 60°\) (the \(+_\theta\) output is kept), then through SG₃ oriented along \(x\). What fraction of these atoms leaves through the \(+_x\) output of SG₃?

Solution

\(\ket{+_z} = \cos\frac\theta2\ket{+_\theta} + \sin\frac\theta2\ket{-_\theta}\), so \(P(+_\theta) = \cos^2\frac\theta2\). Then \(\ket{+_\theta}\) enters \(\mathrm{SG}_x\): \(P(+_x) = \cos^2\frac{90° - \theta}{2} = \frac12(1 + \sin\theta)\). In total, \(\cos^2\frac\theta2\cdot\frac12(1 + \sin\theta)\).

Exercise 2.10

BB84 in practice

★★★

Alice sends (basis, bit): (Z,0) (X,1) (X,0) (Z,1) (Z,1) (X,0) (Z,0) (X,1) (Z,1) (X,1) (Z,0) (X,0). Bob measures in: Z X Z X Z Z X X Z X X Z. Eve intercepts qubits 3, 6 and 11 and measures them in the Z basis. What error rate (QBER, between 0 and 1) do Alice and Bob observe on their sifted key?

Solution

The bases agree for qubits 1, 2, 5, 8, 9 and 10. Sifted key: 0 1 1 1 1 1. Qubits 3, 6 and 11 are discarded during sifting, so Eve introduces no visible error: QBER = 0.

Had Eve intercepted qubit 2 (Alice in X, Bob in X), her measurement in Z would have given Bob a wrong bit with probability ½. This is why Alice and Bob sacrifice part of the sifted key to estimate the QBER: an eavesdropper who intercepts everything produces 25% errors on average.

Clarification

In the statement in the lecture notes (exercise 2.5.4), the three qubits intercepted by Eve are precisely those where Bob did not choose Alice’s basis. The question “what QBER gives Eve away?” therefore has the answer 0: the example does not show detection. To illustrate it, Eve must intercept at least one kept qubit, for example qubit 2 or 8.

·Where do things stand in 2026?

A quantum key between Beijing and Stellenbosch

The Chinese microsatellite Jinan-1 distributed quantum keys in real time to mobile ground stations of about 100 kg. It enabled encrypted communication between Beijing and Stellenbosch (South Africa), 12,900 km apart. The satellite acts as a trusted relay: it knows the keys, so this is not end-to-end entanglement. The security of BB84 is proven for the ideal protocol; real devices have their own weaknesses.

Li et al., Nature (19 March 2025); press release of the Chinese Academy of Sciences
Measuring without destroying everything: error correction

A quantum error-correcting code repeatedly measures mutually commuting operators, chosen to signal an error without reading the stored information. At the end of 2024, Google showed on its Willow chip that the error of a logical qubit (surface code) is divided by about 2.14 each time the code distance is increased. Not to be confused with mere error detection, where faulty runs are discarded.

Nature 638, 920 (2025)
A clock read out by another ion

NIST’s aluminium-ion clock reaches a relative uncertainty of \(5.5\times10^{-19}\). The Al+ ion is hard to read out directly: its state is transferred to a neighbouring Mg+ ion, which is then measured. A decision to redefine the second is not expected before the 2030 General Conference on Weights and Measures.

Physical Review Letters, July 2025; BIPM
